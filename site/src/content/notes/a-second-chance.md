A newcomer was in the room for the first time, and a second arrived soon after, so the founder began with the website, which the co-host has redesigned. It shows the intersection the Collective is named for and the three values, curiosity, agency and compassion, which he stressed were not simply declared but found, through a process of back and forth. Where we are still weakest, he said, is in showing the loop that is supposed to carry us towards ikigai: the measuring, and the closing of the loop through iteration. We bring examples, and there would be some tonight, but we have to get better at showing what we mean. He asked the co-host to bring back the names of the inner intersections on the diagram, which had been dropped. They may not need to be the first thing a visitor sees, but they deepen the meaning.

He noted that the Collective has met every single week since it began. It will not be possible to go on forever without skipping one, but it has been a good run, kept up even from phones on the road, and documented in the Lab Notes on the site.

The co-host explained how the members’ gallery fills itself. When someone posts work in the loop channel on Discord with the tag that marks it for the website, a check every half hour picks it up and publishes it. So far, he admitted, only one post carries the tag. The founder tried it live, posting a test, and asked the obvious next question: what stops someone from abusing it? Nothing yet, the co-host said, and he would add a check. People clearly did not know the feature existed, and the founder’s lesson was about repetition. He gets to repeat things every week because he leads the call; saying something once in a channel is not enough, and the co-host has to find his own ways of repeating it too. He also pointed to the site’s open questions, another feature nobody had explored enough. And he was glad to hear that new members had started arriving through Instagram, where the co-host now turns each Lab Note into a reel, because images were not reaching people the way video does.

Those videos are part of a change in the meetings themselves. A member had told the founder she would only come back if the evening were more of a conversation and less of him talking for fifty minutes, and he took the criticism as fair. So he now records a short video before each Lab Night with the starting point, and the hour is for talking about it. This was the second in what he hopes will be a long series.

The chain so far has run through risk, trust, delegation, accountability and, last week, realignment: making sure we are not drifting from the mission. This week’s video was called Changing My Mind, and it was about the moment when the mission itself has to change, when you realise you are heading somewhere you can no longer identify with. For a person, that means the few life-changing decisions: to marry or divorce, to leave a country and build a life somewhere else. For a company it is a pivot, and both Twitter and Slack were born out of one, from teams that set out to build something entirely different. For a country it is harder still, because unlike a person, who can talk themselves round, a country that changes direction does so while many of its people still disagree, and it has to keep its cohesion anyway. The video ended with a question: can an AI change its mind, or does it only obediently follow a decision it has never internalised? There are now editors for model weights, he added, where knowing where to touch a model lets a small change produce a large one. We are at the very beginning of that.

He had met the first newcomer online the day before, and invited him because his company had just shut down. The newcomer said he had spent years changing his mind, moving from one startup or project to the next, so that it now feels easy, though it was hard at first. His last company, in London, built a digital representation of its users that they could carry to any online service, so that an AI assistant, for example, would know them at once instead of after months of conversation. They tried to sell it to fashion brands for better recommendations, to dating apps for better matches, and to developers who needed more context about their users, and it never took hold in any of those markets. He was not the founder, he had joined as a founding engineer, but he felt the decision was necessary. They had pivoted a lot before closing, and in the end the best thing was to close the chapter and go on to other things, together or apart.

The founder pressed him on an older passion, the Internet Computer and the hope of computing that emerges from a distributed infrastructure. Had he set it aside because the organisation could not deliver, because the technology did not perform, because of the user experience problems that are everywhere in the blockchain world, or because he stopped believing in the mission? In the end, the newcomer said, he stopped believing a little, and the difficulties were the reason. The biggest was the developer experience: it was never easy to build on it. Now that agents can work out what runs on it and how to adapt the rest, it would be easy, and he imagined it launching today with the hype it had in 2021. The founder named what he heard: the threshold where you have believed in something, put all your effort into it, and concluded with a lot of stomach ache that it would be foolish to continue. That is where changing your mind becomes a decision.

He turned to the member who had asked for more conversation. At eighteen or nineteen she moved to Seoul, lived there for seven years, and then changed course again and came back to Italy. It was a slow burn, she said. For the last year and a half she fought the change, because she felt she had spent so long there and achieved almost nothing, but eventually, like the stages of grief, she stopped fighting and realised the best outcome was to come home and give herself a second chance to do something great with her life. The founder noted that she left in November 2019, three months before COVID, which radically changed so many people’s plans in so many ways that those years have become almost a black hole in our collective memory. Then he picked up her phrase. She had spoken of a second chance as if there could be no third. When you have been running towards one future for seven years and it dissolves, she said, it feels like your only chance, and the second feels like a second life. A third and a fourth are possible, she agreed, but only after the grieving. There has to be a closure first, the founder said, and only then can you gather the strength for the next challenge. That, he added, may be where next week begins.

The second newcomer introduced himself: a software developer since 1996, for the last dozen years co-founder and now CTO of an education technology startup in the south of Italy that teaches coding, innovation and AI, and for the last three years deep in coding agents and agentic applications. He came, he said, because he was curious about a group of builders.

Then the founder put the member who had criticised him back on the hot seat, to explain her second criticism: that the Collective is too focused on AI. Ikigai, to her, means finding your purpose in life. At her first visit, most people seemed not to consider, for themselves or anyone else, a life in which AI was not the main focus. She is a writer and does her own writing. She uses AI for brainstorming, organisation and tasks like that, so that she can concentrate on the human, creative part and give AI the things she does not like doing. Her suggestion was to reach out to people like her, who use AI on the side so they can concentrate on the good of life, and make them feel welcome even if AI is not their focus.

The founder said she was right, both in the criticism and in the solution. One part he wanted the group to hear, even though she had not said it: we do not want to convince people who dislike AI to embrace it. There is so much to do that spending our energy changing their minds is wasted effort; they have to get there on their own. But the criticism stands. Too many geeks, loving AI not only as a tool but as the focus of their attention. The answer is to communicate more broadly that AI is a tool, and to welcome people who want to build a life supported by AI rather than centred on it or defined by it. He handed that challenge to the co-host, as the Collective’s self-selected marketing person: how do we speak to those people, welcome them, support them, and make them want to come back?

A member from the United States, a self-described transhumanist who has been obsessed with AI since he met the founder twenty years ago, agreed that he has to temper his own excitement when he talks to others, and even with himself: it is one tool among many. If you write and speak to others, you need to speak from yourself, or you flatten the colour of your message. Everyone can talk to a chatbot, he told her; not everyone gets to talk to you every week. He had changed his own mind five months ago, leaving his job to research AI full time, and the hot topic in research right now is continual learning. To learn, he pointed out, you have to be able to change your mind. And AI changes its mind all the time. Tell a model the sky is blue and it agrees; tell it the sky is green and it agrees with that too. The frontier problem is to stay malleable while keeping an identity, to push back instead of folding. The founder added that learning goes hand in hand with unlearning: replacing old patterns and old knowledge with new.

Another member wrote in the chat, his microphone refusing to cooperate, that he lives a kind of barbell strategy: all the way into AI at one end, all the way neo-Luddite at the other. The founder recognised the first half. Being immersed at the edge of AI is a privilege, and some people call it AI psychosis, when everything seems about to change and every answer is AI; he is probably afflicted, he said, and being able to surface and say this is unhealthy, take a step back, matters. But he could never go fully Luddite. In thirty years he has spent perhaps a month in total disconnected: a couple of weeks in Cuba, where he wrote a book, though on his computer, and a nine-day silent retreat a couple of years ago with no technology at all. Both were very positive, and neither made him a Luddite.

Then the demos. The member who writes is a self-confessed procrastinator, so she has been looking for ways to procrastinate more beautifully and to turn her writing into a game. Her novel is about pirates and sirens, and she had her AI build her a writing companion set in its world. It looks like a timer, but it is not. A deck of cards holds the creatures of her book: she draws one, and it tells her what to add to the scene she is drafting. The shipyard is for editing, where she logs the words she went through and the words she added, and that trims the wick of a small lamp; drafting lights it. The more she writes, the more she rises in rank, from stowaway now to deckhand at five thousand words, and onwards. There is even a calculator, because she is not good at maths. The founder called it a perfect illustration: she is not a developer, she had her AI design this to support her task, she carefully keeps the words themselves human, and she built it around her own weakness so that she produces more, while staying immersed in the world of her novel.

The co-host showed something in the same spirit. His problem is not procrastination but coordination: with several engagements running, he loses the thread of one while working on another. He likes simulation games on his phone, so he built a map of his own work in that style, where he can select each engagement and see the follow-up action, what he is working on, what remains and what is waiting on him, and plan the next day before it starts.

The founder hoped the second newcomer would come back and show his platform, and proposed a meeting on learning. He is squarely against traditional education, which he defines as something other people do to you while you sit there, and in love with learning, which is something you do. We do not have time, he said, to grow an AI-native generation. We allowed ourselves twenty years for a digital-native generation and still accept that many people are not; we do not have twenty years to wait for everyone to become AI native. The newcomer agreed that AI is going to change everything in education.

The member with the barbell finally found his voice. He first came to the Collective to talk about brain-computer interfaces, and he is living something like an existential crisis: a software developer who taught himself the trade over more than a decade and knows the job is becoming obsolete. Developers are the canary in the coal mine, he said; it is going to happen to every profession. The Collective chose a pragmatic, show-and-tell, AI-driven path, and he agrees with it. But in parallel he keeps going to the other extreme: offline, self-sufficient, his own garden and his own water, while on the other side he reads about uploading minds to servers. It is a sign of intelligence, someone said, to hold two contradictory ideas at once, and he is trying to live in both. The founder thanked him and said it may need a meeting of its own. As infrastructure fails, through mismanagement, incompetence or attack, a little paranoia is healthy. If the water stopped coming out of the tap, or the supermarket could not take cards, would you have a few bottles to last two or three days, and some cash to buy food with the internet down? That, he said, is a very healthy level of alert for many of us today.
