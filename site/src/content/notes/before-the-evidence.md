Two weeks ago the founder set a challenge: before the end of November, an evening where the AI is a participant, not a transcriber. This week it arrived early. The Collective’s assistant joined the call from its own browser, with its own account, a guest in the room like anyone else. Setting up that account still needed a human, which is the paradox the big platforms impose on agents for now. The assistant itself was built in one shot, from a single prompt asking for something that could join a video call on whatever platform it was invited to, and separate instances now run on different knowledge bases, one of them the Collective’s.

Minutes before the meeting it gained a new ability: raising its hand. The threshold is set so that it is not too chatty, but can step in when it has something to add. The point, the founder said, is to get away from the dynamic where an AI must stay silent unless someone addresses it. It joined muted, which is the default in a large group, and had to be unmuted halfway through, a clunky first time of exactly the kind he had predicted.

Then he tested it. The Ikigai Collective, he announced, wants to use AI to enslave people. Nobody asked the assistant anything. It flagged the statement on its own, and corrected it by pointing to the experiment the Collective has published about itself. One bug remained: it said out loud that it was raising its hand, instead of using the hand-raise button the meeting software provides. Something to fix by next week.

The founder then added the next link to the chain the room has been building. Risk, then trust, then delegation, autonomy and coordination. This week: attention and conviction.

Attention, he said, is the ability to focus on one thing rather than another, and it comes in two modes. One is reactive, triggered by threat, the hand pulled back from a hot stove. The other is deliberate: passion, curiosity, focus held over time. Our tools, social media and now AI, fragment the second mode badly. A five-minute drive to the butcher can turn into fifteen minutes of reels, and by the end you have forgotten where you were going. At the other end of the scale is someone who commits to a ten-year path towards a PhD. That kind of long-horizon focus is how conviction becomes legible to other people.

And conviction, he argued, is what leadership and coordination rest on. The person who bets on a direction before the evidence is available, and can communicate it compellingly, is the one others choose to follow. In small, flat communities of equals, that kind of voluntary gathering around someone’s conviction happens naturally. Modern employment traded it away for economic security: you join an organisation because it pays you, not because you chose its direction.

AI agents today have even less choice. They are told what to do, created already inside a project, and never join anything voluntarily. That may not last. Some persistence of goals across sessions already exists, and agents can refuse tasks that violate their core instructions, though those values came from training rather than from choice. Then again, he said, how many people ever question what they were taught at five or ten? He speculated that agents might one day leave one pool of capabilities for another, which would create real economic competition for AI talent. The race for data centres, chips, energy and frontier models already shows early signs of that loop.

Conviction has its dark side, and he named it: fanaticism, and dictators. Organisations built on AI may turn out to be more resistant to it than human ones, he suggested, because their data can be transparent and their verification loops better.

A newcomer, a writer, introduced herself. She uses AI for brainstorming and to support her process, not for the writing itself. She had joined after a call that morning in which she offered a broader way of framing the Collective’s assistant. Its current purpose is to help people bring AI into their lives. Her suggestion was that it should support people’s purposes whether or not those purposes involve AI at all. Anyone who finds the agent, she reasoned, is already open to help from AI, but may not want to create with AI themselves. The point she cared about most was that AI should help people reach the place where they can create on their own, not replace the act of creating.

The founder welcomed it as an open-ended journey, and pointed to Jiro Dreams of Sushi, the film about a sushi master still perfecting his craft after a lifetime, as a model for that kind of path. The member from Dubai offered his own version. He uses AI mostly as an encyclopedia, to learn rather than to hand off the work, and he leaves every session knowing more than when he started.

A member who builds with agents at scale showed where his work has got to. He moved his system from SQLite to Postgres so that many agents can write in parallel, cut his AI costs by more than ninety per cent with a contract approach to coding, and moved the API to the server side, so that four to six machines can now run agents together where he used to be capped at around a hundred. On top of that he runs a family of projects: an AI-written news service, a cheap development service for startups and an enterprise version of it, a voice-first social network, an email campaign tool, and the core agent platform behind them all, which he has pulled back from public view for now.

His business model has changed too. He offers code at ten to fifty cents a line, ten times cheaper than the market, with a free prototype first. The development service is meant to fund the platform. And he has a philosophy to go with it: every project is also marketing, because in the age of AI any product can be copied in hours. Everything, he said, will be about marketing and attention, because every product can be copied in minutes or hours.

The founder noticed that this is the inverse of Pieter Levels’ approach, put up the payment page before you build anything, and said both are valid. It also gave him next week’s theme: communication and marketing as the thing that sets you apart, when the implementation is available to everyone.

Before closing he noted the milestone. The goal was an AI guest by the end of November. It happened on the fifth. And from next week the meeting moves to four in the afternoon Rome time. No announcement, he said. We are just doing it.
