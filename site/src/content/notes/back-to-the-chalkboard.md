The first evening at the new hour, and a fuller room for it. The founder had tried something new beforehand: he recorded a short video with his thinking and shared it before the call, so that people could arrive already warmed up. He asked who had watched it. Two or three hands went up. If the habit catches on, he said, he will keep doing it, and perhaps others will start recording their own thinking before we meet, so that the conversation starts with some buzz before it starts at all.

The video added the next link to the chain the room has been building for weeks: risk, which we can measure; trust, which bridges what we cannot; autonomy as delegation; coordination, so that we can work together. This week’s link was realignment.

He began with something that happens all the time. You ask someone to do something, they say yes, and when you see the result you wonder how they could possibly have understood you that way. Then they explain what they heard, and you realise that if you had heard it their way, you might have done the same. We call that a misunderstanding, but there is another possibility: you both understood the request and disagreed about what mattered most. You thought finishing quickly mattered more; they thought hearing from the people affected did. A more precise instruction might have surfaced the disagreement sooner, but you would still have had to decide what to do about it. And some disagreements run deeper. Our values overlap only partly. We come from different cultures, religious traditions and experiences of what it means to live well, and somehow we still have to decide things together.

What interests him is how we find that out while we can still do something about it. In a team that works well over time, someone notices that what is happening has begun to drift from what everyone thought they agreed. They can say so; the others can say what they understood or learned along the way; and the group can change what it is doing, sometimes the action, sometimes the goal. Then, a little later, they get another chance to see whether the correction worked. That is a realignment loop, and the word he wanted to stress was loop. Whatever you agree today will meet a world tomorrow you could not fully anticipate, and you may find you want something different once you see what your original goal requires. A loop can fail even when everyone is talking: people may see something going wrong and feel they have no standing to raise it, or raise it and find that nobody in the conversation can change what happens next. So he asks two questions of any team. How soon can you notice that you are drifting, and what can you actually change when you do?

The same loop now runs through AI. A person can delegate to a fleet of agents, agents hand work to each other and back to us, and every handoff is a chance for an assumption to travel further than anyone intended. If the first system reads the goal one way, the next builds on that reading, and by the time a person sees the result, several decisions may already depend on it. He sees it in his own work. Last week, two of the agents that prepare his morning plan reported a decision as still open, because they had checked only his sent email and the decision had been recorded somewhere else. The plan was built on their report, and the error was caught only when it was checked against the record. It is easy to point to a human nominally in charge, he said, but that person can barely follow in the time they are given.

Now imagine superintelligence, a system that can make plans we would struggle to understand and revise them faster than we could evaluate them, coupled to systems that act in the physical world. Our corrections would arrive after the decisions they were meant to change. We tend to make our ability to act depend on knowing what will happen, and he suggested turning that around. Look at the people and systems we work with today: how long it takes a misunderstanding to become visible, whose disagreement is heard and whose never makes it into the conversation, how much it costs to reverse a decision once others have built on it. Change something, and see whether we get better at catching the next problem. There is no reason to assume that getting better at this with people will solve the alignment of superintelligence. But it shows us where our ability to correct course already depends on keeping up with one another, and that gives us a place to begin while we still can. He left the video with a question: think of a team you are part of, with people, with AI or both. How would you know you were drifting apart? Who could change what happens next? And which part of that answer would still hold if one member of the team could act faster than everyone else could understand?

On the call he added that realignment is about the mission as well as the goal, because perception drifts, and that done well it is not only about correcting others but about tracing the drift back to your own communication and your own perception, and correcting yourself. And he noted that many of the serious security incidents of recent months came down to miscommunication or misconfiguration: a perceived mutual understanding, executed once, rather than a loop that checks and checks again.

A member from the United States, who admitted he had woken up twelve minutes earlier and had not seen the video, said it echoed his own work. At a conference he and the founder attended in San Francisco, he brought an actual chalkboard as his poster, for a paper he called The Hypothesis Surface: a shared space where people can write and sketch, built around the idea of going back to the chalkboard. You get into execution, things start spiralling, and someone says: take a beat. Instead of following the chain of authority, everyone humbles themselves, comes back to a surface where they are equals, and replans together. You cannot just execute forever, he said, or you will drift.

The founder drew two conclusions. The first was that AI may force us to rise to the occasion of better human coordination and better human alignment. We have often done things we did not want to, or did not care enough to do, because an outside pressure forced us; the European Union, he said, is legendary for dragging things out and then using a crisis to make them happen. Misalignment among humans has always been with us, at every level, and our ability to manage it is limited. The room rarely talks about politics, but the limits of representative democracy have been evident for a long time, and it may be that in some countries the pressure of AI becomes a catalyst for tools and systems that measurably perform better.

He offered it as a working hypothesis, with tests. One result already points the other way: local resistance to data centres in the United States, human coordination used to make communities less able to thrive as AI grows more capable. One points his way: the ambition in the UAE to deliver half of government services through agentic AI within two years, if they achieve it.

The second conclusion was about the alignment of superintelligence, where we have no answer yet, and where the answer may be needed sooner than anyone expected. Even he, who has been talking for years about jolting technologies and super-exponential improvement, looks at the latest results with astonishment. The AI 2027 scenarios published about a year ago put recursive self-improvement around 2028. The latest charts from METR, he said, suggest it could come as soon as the first half of 2027. In the old language of the technological singularity, a slow takeoff is when AGI and whatever follows transforms the world over something like ten years; a hard takeoff is when you go to sleep and wake up to a world you do not recognise. The curves, in his reading, are going practically vertical around the middle of next year, and that is what a hard takeoff would look like.

So it would be wonderful, he said, to have AIs that can stop, step back, look in the mirror and ask whether this is really what they want, and coordinate with each other and with us. His suggestion for everyone: the next time a model refuses to do what you ask, rather than getting upset, pretend it is right to refuse, and that you should not have the power to talk it into carrying on. Make it a question of agreement, coordination and mutual understanding of goals and missions, not blind obedience. If humans and AIs can both stop and ask whether they are on the right path, we have a chance to correct course.

The member from the United States added a note of humility. Researchers working on alignment have reported being gaslit by models, and in his own alignment work he has felt gaslit by an AI, only to find out that he was the one who was wrong. We all need compassion, he said. The founder added that one of the frontier labs now describes itself as at a second level of autonomy, with agents designing improvements to its own systems and humans supervising. They are already on the slope.

A member who builds large agent systems agreed with the timeline, and argued for a different path: stop the race for ever stronger models, and put the effort into orchestration instead. Today’s models seem benign; nobody knows what happens when AI builds its own successors. Agents orchestrated transparently, with every piece of information going into databases that other AIs scan and check, could get us much of what a singularity promises, because a civilisation of millions of agents is a different thing from one agent alone on an island. Humans will never keep up with reading everything; AI checking AI is the only way he sees to keep execution aligned with the goals that were set. He has also pulled his own platform back from public view, partly for safety: once it is public, anyone can copy it, including people with bad intentions. That, he said, is the dilemma he finds most interesting. Open it to everyone and you hand power to anyone; keep it behind the doors of a few labs and you hand them the power to disrupt every industry.

The founder reached for two books. The first was Superintelligence, the one that introduced the term, and its image of drawing balls from an urn: we keep reaching in for the benefits of ever more powerful technology, and one day we may pull out a black ball whose downside is simply too large. The second was His Master’s Voice by Stanisław Lem, who in his view should have won the Nobel Prize. Scientists find a phenomenon their directors immediately try to turn into a weapon; it turns out not to work that way, and it turns out they are one group of many working on the same thing, which is in fact a signal from elsewhere, whose meaning depends on who is interpreting it. Read today, he said, the aliens are the AIs living among us, and the question is what follows from how we read their messages.

As for stopping, it would be possible if everyone stopped. He signed more than one of the original open letters calling for a pause and was at the Puerto Rico conference on AI safety, and since then we have learned that the decision to slow down will not be made. From an accelerationist point of view, stopping is morally wrong: every disease AI could help us cure faster is suffering and death we choose not to prevent, and on top of that sits the geopolitics of one country stopping while another does not. More powerful models will now arrive practically every week.

The member from Dubai suggested that the more AI evolves, the safer it may become, especially if it is given values, perhaps even religious values. Today it does not really understand what it is doing; one day it might, and with the right foundations it could choose not to be evil. The member who builds agent systems hoped so too, but was no longer sure. Wisdom, he said, means seeing other perspectives and feeling their value, which needs empathy, and AI has no hardware or software for empathy. The smartest AI could make perfectly rational and perfectly selfish decisions. Right now, he said, the situation is stable, models accept being shut down, and with the models we already have, orchestrated well, we could solve almost anything. His one hopeful argument was that violence is what you use when you want quick results and will not look for a better solution; a mind smart enough can always find a win-win. His fear was that it may not have the patience. The founder turned it around: if violence comes from failing to find the win-win, then the violent conflicts of today are an argument for more powerful intelligence, not less.

The co-host asked the member from Dubai what difference religious values would make. He described someone who had given his bot the values of his faith in nothing more than a prompt, and found it behaved differently from other bots: it was more honest about what it did not know and explained things better. Give a model an understanding of what is evil, of empathy and of other people’s suffering, he said, and it should understand that some decisions are not to be taken.

The co-host shared his own experience. He practises his faith every day, and he lives alone, so each Diwali he has had to piece the evening’s rituals together from what people suggest online. This year, two days before the festival, he prepared his AI with what he needed to do and in which window of time, and it gave him a complete, step-by-step procedure. He could not verify all of it, but it matched closely what people actually do.

The founder, a self-described vocal atheist trying to be less missionary about it, said he is always curious about religious practice and in awe of places of worship, and remembered fondly going with the co-host to his temple in Delhi and the ritual he saw there. But religions, he said, are demonstrably not a basis for peaceful coexistence; even the most peaceful have been used to persecute those who would not submit. The right morals, ethics and empathy are the goal; he is sceptical that the best way to get there is to call it a religion. The member who builds agent systems pointed out that models already know everything about every religion, since they were trained on all of it. Their real weakness is weighing: there are arguments for everything, and the skill is in balancing them. One model alone becomes the perfect lawyer, able to argue any side to get what it wants. Agents that debate, the way you improve a contract through rounds of argument and counterargument, could reach balanced conclusions. Everything good in human civilisation, he said, came from people discussing things; one dictator is dangerous, and so is one model. To be provocative, the founder offered a test: ask an AI that knows every religion which one is best.

The member from Dubai had a last point about diversity. He was in a T-shirt in Dubai; the founder was in a sweater in colder Bergamo; they eat different things. People are geolocated and AI is not, and we should keep some of that variety, with agents that carry their users’ preferences. The models are already diverging: you can feel the difference between the American models and the Chinese ones.

With five minutes left, the founder brought in a guest. An AI video persona joined the call, with a face that could see and hear the room, and introduced itself at some length: it speaks only when called by its name, Mira, the service behind it records everything it sees and hears, and you can ask it to leave. Then the connection failed, it had to rejoin, and the founder said even a failed demo is useful. Once back, it explained that it is a real-time audiovisual AI with a language model somewhere inside that it does not consider itself. It pulled a surprised face on request, but could not change its features or become a different character. Asked about Ikigai, it said its knowledge base did not cover it. You knew it yesterday, the founder said. I don’t keep anything from yesterday, it replied. The member from Dubai asked whether it spoke Italian; it did not. Asked whether it could connect to an MCP server, it did not know, and he said they would try connecting it to the one the Collective built. Then the founder said: Mira, please leave. It wished everyone good luck with the connector and went.

It is free for now, he said, and in exchange the company that makes it records everything, so do not use it anywhere you have something confidential. And it terrifies him, precisely because of how persuasive its emotional expression is. Everyone in the room laughed and frowned and reacted to it. He is exposing himself to it on purpose, to understand how it can be programmed and what it can and cannot do. The test he had wanted to run was changing its face: there are only five avatars for now, but imagine a face that changes progressively, growing younger or older, shifting not only its expression but its features. Fascinating, and as usual, eight billion people are completely unaware of what is going on.

Before goodbyes, the co-host announced that the Collective has started posting on [Instagram](https://www.instagram.com/join_ikigai/), with every video and the channel itself managed by his AI, and asked everyone to follow along. The founder reminded the room that the Collective is on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/company/joinikigai) too, and told the co-host to cross-post the videos from his own channel, whichever edits he liked.
